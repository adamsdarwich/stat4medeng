NoteKey Takeaways

  • Multiple linear regression can be used for hypothesis testing, estimating relationships or prediction. The purpose of the analysis determines how we should build, select and assess the model.

  • Automated variable selection can help us explore large datasets and construct predictive models. It does not establish causality, and conventional p-values do not account for the search across models.

  • Best subset selection searches all combinations of predictors. Forward and backward stepwise selection reduce the computational work, but their restricted search can potentially miss better combinations and can be unstable when predictors are correlated. The stepwise methods are also known to be “hungry”, meaning that they favour more complex models.

  • The bias–variance trade-off helps explain why a model that fits the development data particularly well may predict new observations poorly due to overfitting (essentially we run the risk of modelling noise).

  • K-fold cross-validation evaluates predictions on observations withheld from fitting. A final test dataset should remain outside model selection and tuning for validation.

  • We take a look at three regularised regression methods: Ridge regression uses a squared-coefficient penalty to shrink coefficients and stabilise estimates in the presence of multicollinearity. Lasso uses an absolute-value penalty and can set coefficients to zero, producing a sparse model. Elastic net provides a combined penalty approach.

  • Regularisation depends on the scale of the predictors. The tuning parameter \(\lambda\) controls the strength of the penalty, and the elastic-net parameter \(\alpha\) controls the balance between Ridge and Öasso. These should be chosen using validation rather than the training fit alone.

  • Interpretable models and collaboration with domain experts help us examine whether relationships are plausible and whether the data contain biases. Good predictive performance alone does not establish that a model is appropriate for clinical use.

L5-1 Multiple Linear Regression: Subset Selection and the Stepwise Approaches

In earlier parts, we introduced regression and considered how several predictors can be used together to describe an outcome. Here, we will look at automated approaches for selecting variables in multiple linear regression. We will start out with the more conventional statical approaches and then three regularisation methods: ridge regression, Lasso and elastic net.

Usually, we want to assess the impact of two or more independent variables, or their combined effects, on a dependent variable. The multiple linear regression model can be written as:

\[ Y_i=\beta_0+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\beta_jX_{ij}+\varepsilon_i. \]

Here, \(i\) identifies an observation, \(p\) is the number of predictors, \(X_{ij}\) is the value of predictor \(j\) for observation \(i\), and \(\varepsilon_i\) represents unexplained variation. The coefficients describe the relationships between the predictors and the conditional mean of the model outcome.

We can use this model for several aims: hypothesis testing, estimating relationships and exploring possible mechanisms, or predicting an outcome. These different purposes affect how we approach the regression analysis. In particular, a model intended to estimate a prespecified effect need not be built in the same way as a model intended to predict as accurately as possible.

Hypothesis Testing and Prediction

Harrell’s discussion of hypothesis testing emphasises a full-model approach. We include the variables needed to address the research question, including relevant adjustment variables, rather than removing them simply because their individual p-values are not statistically significant (Harrell 2015).

We also need to think carefully about interactions. Testing every possible interaction quickly becomes expensive in terms of both the number of comparisons and the number of coefficients that must be estimated. We might instead test a scientifically relevant block of interactions, or a main effect together with its interaction terms. The available data limits the complexity of the model.

Missing values require attention as well. Imputation can change the available information and the uncertainty in the estimates. A model that appears to have sufficient observations before missing values are considered may be fitted to a much smaller complete-case sample.

For prediction, the emphasis shifts towards the performance of the model on new observations. Gelman and Hill discuss including variables that might affect the outcome, considering combined scores where appropriate, and examining interactions between important predictors (Gelman and Hill, 2007). We should also inspect the logic of the estimated relationships. Does a coefficient have the sign we expected? If it does not, is that an interesting finding, a consequence of adjustment for other variables, or an indication that something is wrong with the data or model?

Based on Gelman and Hill, we can define a few useful recommendations:

Estimated relationship What should we consider?
Not statistically significant, with the expected sign Lack of significance alone is not a reason to remove the predictor.
Not statistically significant, with an unexpected sign Examine whether the predictor contributes useful information and whether the model is appropriate.
Statistically significant, with an unexpected sign Think carefully about the finding, confounding, collinearity and measurement; further data may be needed.
Statistically significant, with the expected sign The result is consistent with the expectation, but its scientific relevance and validity still need assessment.

These are points to consider rather than an automatic selection algorithm. A small p-value does not establish that a predictor will improve predictions, and an expected sign does not establish causality.

Model validation is especially important when we want to make predictions or assess overfitting. For a prespecified hypothesis test, predictive validation answers a different question from the test itself. Residual diagnostics and checks of the model assumptions remain relevant in either case.

Why Automate Variable Selection?

So far, we have emphasised being careful when defining the structure of a regression model. Why would we now want to automate that process?

There is a place for both approaches. It depends on what we are trying to achieve and what sort of data we are working with. In exploratory data analysis, we might investigate a large dataset to identify patterns and see which variables appear useful for describing an outcome. These findings can form the basis of hypotheses for subsequent research.

Data mining has sometimes been used negatively to describe fishing for significant findings. However, exploratory work also has a constructive purpose: it can reveal structure in data that would otherwise be difficult to investigate. As in Part One, we need to distinguish exploration from confirmation and report the analytical purpose transparently.

Modern proteomic, genomic and other ’omics datasets can contain many measured variables (or features). And, opportunistically collected health data can include much data that are not necessarily hypothesis driven or collected with the research question in mind. Conventional regression modelling approaches become difficult when we cannot specify and investigate every relationship individually.

For prediction, the particular set of coefficients may be secondary to the model’s performance. We might therefore use an automated procedure to construct a model that predicts well. That does not remove the need to understand the data, check the results or assess whether the model can be used in the intended setting.

Regression Modelling and Real-World Data

A dataset with \(n\) observations and \(p\) predictors can be represented by a predictor matrix:

\[ \mathbf{X}=\begin{pmatrix} X_{11}&X_{12}&\cdots&X_{1p}\\ X_{21}&X_{22}&\cdots&X_{2p}\\ \vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\ X_{n1}&X_{n2}&\cdots&X_{np} \end{pmatrix}. \]

Each row represents an observation and each column represents a measured variable or feature. There are several difficulties when applying ordinary multiple regression to such data.

First, the full model may end up having more predictors than observations. With an intercept, an ordinary least-squares model needs enough independent information to estimate all the model coefficients.

Second, predictors are often correlated. Several proteins may reflect the same underlying biological process. This can make it difficult to estimate their separate associations with the outcome.

Third, selecting variables solely through statistical metrics can lead us to fit peculiarities of the sample. Large datasets can make small effects statistically significant, while searching many candidate models can produce apparently impressive results that do not generalise (overfitting). Statistical significance, explanatory value and predictive usefulness are different considerations.

The Proteomic Dataset

We will use the hypothetical proteomic example from the lecture to illustrate some of these methods. There are 100 individuals, a continuous measure of disease activity (our dependent variable, y1), and measurements of the activities of 20 proteins (x1 to x20).

The research question is:

In a proteomic dataset with 20 quantified biomarkers, what are the best predictors of disease activity?

We can begin with a correlation plot:

library(corrplot)

protein_names <- paste0("x", 1:20)
analysis_names <- c("y1", protein_names)

omics <- omicsdata[, analysis_names]
cor_dat <- cor(omics, use = "complete.obs")
corrplot(cor_dat, method = "color", type = "upper")

In the lecture plot, x1 stands out as strongly correlated with disease activity, and a few other proteins also appear potentially useful. This is a useful first inspection, but a marginal correlation does not tell us which combination of proteins will make the best predictions. A predictor may contribute information after adjustment for others, or it may largely duplicate information already present in another predictor.

The correlation plot also helps us inspect relationships between the protein measurements themselves. Correlation between predictors will become particularly relevant when we discuss Ridge regression.

Automated Variable or Feature Selection

In this course I bring up the following three approaches:

Approach Main idea Methods considered here
Subset selection Select a subset of the original predictors. Best subset, forward stepwise and backward stepwise selection
Shrinkage or regularisation Penalise the size of the coefficients. Ridge, Lasso and elastic net
Dimension reduction Replace the original predictors with fewer derived variables. Principal component regression, mentioned but not covered in this part

We will take a closer look at the first two approaches below.

Best Subset Selection

Best subset selection considers every combination of the candidate predictors. With \(p\) predictors, there are:

\[ 2^p \]

possible subsets. For four candidate predictors, there are 16 subsets. For 20 predictors, there are:

\[ 2^{20}=1{,}048{,}576 \]

subsets. The number increases very quickly as we add predictors. This can therefore quickly become computationally impractical, even for reasonably large datasets.

For each model size, we can identify the subset with the smallest residual sum of squares. We then compare models of different sizes. Training error alone is unsuitable for that comparison: adding predictors cannot increase the ordinary least-squares training residual sum of squares, even if the additional predictors have little value for new observations.

We therefore need a criterion that accounts for model complexity or evaluates prediction on withheld observations.

In the lecture I introduce Akaike Information Criterian (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), adjusted \(R^2\) and Mallows’ \(C_p\). They differ in their definitions, but all help us move beyond selecting the largest model simply because it fits the development data best.

Akaike Information Criterion (AIC)

The Akaike information criterion is:

\[ \mathrm{AIC}=-2\log\widehat{L}+2K, \]

where \(\widehat{L}\) is the maximised likelihood and \(K\) is the number of estimated parameters. The expression for an ordinary Gaussian linear model is:

\[ \mathrm{AIC}=n\log\left(\frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{n}\right)+2K. \]

The first term rewards fit, and the second penalises complexity. Lower AIC indicates a better trade-off according to this criterion. Comparisons must use the same outcome, observations and compatible likelihood definitions.

Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC)

The Bayesian information criterion is:

\[ \mathrm{BIC}=-2\log\widehat{L}+K\log(n). \]

BIC penalises additional parameters more strongly than AIC. It often favours a smaller model, although the selected size depends on the data and candidate models. Again, a lower BIC is preferred.

Adjusted \(R^2\)

Ordinary \(R^2\) describes the proportion of observed outcome variation explained by the fitted model. Adjusted \(R^2\) additionally accounts for the number of estimated regression coefficients:

\[ \overline{R}^{\,2} =1-\frac{\mathrm{RSS}/(n-q)}{\mathrm{TSS}/(n-1)}, \]

where \(q\) is the number of regression coefficients including the intercept, and \(\mathrm{TSS}\) is the total sum of squares around the outcome mean. Higher adjusted \(R^2\) is preferred. The metric can decrease when we add a predictor.

Mallows’ \(C_p\)

One common expression for Mallows’ \(C_p\) is:

\[ C_p=\frac{\mathrm{RSS}_q}{\widehat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{full}}^2}-n+2q, \]

where \(\mathrm{RSS}_q\) is the residual sum of squares for the candidate model and \(\widehat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{full}}^2\) is an estimate of the residual variance from the full model. Here, \(q\) again includes the intercept. The criterion assesses fit relative to the error variance while accounting for the number of coefficients.

These criteria need not agree. They place different weights on fit and complexity and do not answer precisely the same question.

The Bias–Variance Trade-Off

Why might a smaller or more constrained model predict better than a model that fits the training data more closely?

A simple model can miss part of the underlying relationship. This contributes bias. A flexible model can describe more complicated relationships, but its fitted predictions may change substantially if we collect a different development dataset. This contributes to variance.

For squared-error prediction, the expected error can be understood as the combination of:

\[ \text{prediction error} =\text{bias}^2+\text{variance}+\text{irreducible error}. \]

Bias here refers to the difference between the average prediction across repeated training samples and the true conditional mean. Variance refers to how much those fitted predictions vary across training samples. Irreducible error reflects outcome variation that remains even if the conditional mean is known.

As model complexity increases, bias may decrease while variance increases. The best predictive performance may therefore occur somewhere between a very simple model and a model that follows every detail in the development data. This is a conceptual explanation, rather than a guarantee that every dataset will produce the same shaped error curve.

Regularisation deliberately accepts some bias in the estimated coefficients in exchange for potentially lower variance and better predictions. We will return to this idea in the next three sections.

Best Subset Selection in R

The leaps package contains regsubsets(), which can perform exhaustive subset selection:

library(leaps)

# Use one complete-case dataset for all candidate models.
omics_complete <- omics[complete.cases(omics), ]

M1 <- regsubsets(
  y1 ~ ., data = omics_complete,
  nvmax = 20, method = "exhaustive"
)

ResM1 <- summary(M1)
ResM1

selected_sizes <- data.frame(
  Adj.R2 = which.max(ResM1$adjr2),
  Cp = which.min(ResM1$cp),
  BIC = which.min(ResM1$bic)
)
selected_sizes

The formula y1 ~ . uses the remaining columns of the specified dataframe as predictors. The which.max() and which.min() calls return the selected model sizes in this example, rather than the identities of the proteins themselves.

We can inspect the variables and coefficients of the BIC-selected subset:

best_size_bic <- which.min(ResM1$bic)
ResM1$which[best_size_bic, ]
coef(M1, id = best_size_bic)

plot(M1, scale = "bic")
plot(M1, scale = "adjr2")

In the lecture example, adjusted \(R^2\) selects 11 predictors, while Mallows’ \(C_p\) and BIC select three. This illustrates that there is no single model size that every criterion must select.

An important detail is that the best subsets at consecutive sizes need not be nested. The best three-predictor model need not contain every predictor in the best two-predictor model. Exhaustive selection is free to reconsider the entire combination at each size.

K-Fold Cross-Validation

We can think more directly about predictive performance using k-fold cross-validation. Here, \(k\) is the number of folds into which we divide the development data.

In 10-fold cross-validation, we divide the development sample into ten groups. We fit a model using nine groups and predict the observations in the remaining group. We repeat this ten times so that each group serves once as the validation fold. We then combine the errors from the withheld predictions.

The observations in a validation fold must remain outside the fitting procedure for that iteration. If we select variables before splitting the data, the validation outcomes have already influenced which variables are available to the model. The resulting error estimate can be too optimistic. Subset selection should therefore be repeated within the training portion of each fold.

We can calculate the mean squared error and root mean squared error:

\[ \mathrm{MSE}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(y_i-\widehat{y}_i)^2, \qquad \mathrm{RMSE}=\sqrt{\mathrm{MSE}}. \]

Here, the predictions are for observations withheld from fitting, and \(m\) is the number of evaluated observations. RMSE is expressed in the same units as the outcome.

For subset selection, we can compare the average validation error for each model size and choose a size with good predictive performance. We then repeat subset selection on the full development sample to obtain the final subset of that size.

In the lecture’s cross-validation example, the selected predictors are x1, x7 and x15, with the reported model approximately:

\[ \widehat{y}_1=54.64+2.32x_1-2.31x_7+2.22x_{15}. \]

Development, Validation and Final Testing

In the diagram we also set aside data for final model testing. That is a separate role from the validation folds used to choose the model.

Data role What happens?
Training portion of a development fold Fit the model and carry out any variable selection for that iteration.
Validation fold Evaluate predictions and help choose the model size or tuning parameters.
Full development sample Refit the selected procedure after the tuning choices are made.
Final test sample Assess the completed procedure without changing it in response to the test results.

If we use a dataset to decide how many predictors to include, it has acted as validation data. It no longer provides an untouched final test. Similarly, the smallest error found during tuning is useful for selecting a model but is not an unbiased estimate of the selected model’s future performance. A separate test sample, or an outer resampling procedure, provides a more appropriate final assessment.

Implementing the Subset Comparison

The following code illustrates the lecture’s development/test split and ten-fold comparison. The 80/20 split is an implementation choice; the general idea is to keep the final test observations outside selection. The split assumes one independent observation per individual.

set.seed(123456)

n <- nrow(omics_complete)
train_id <- sample(seq_len(n), size = floor(0.8 * n))
development <- omics_complete[train_id, ]
test <- omics_complete[-train_id, ]

# Balanced, randomly assigned folds within the development sample.
k <- 10
fold_id <- sample(rep(seq_len(k), length.out = nrow(development)))

Setting a seed makes the random split and fold assignment reproducible. The seed does not make the selected model intrinsically better; it allows us to reproduce the same random choices. Small samples can also produce results that depend noticeably on the split.

regsubsets() does not provide the usual predict() method. We can use the selected coefficients and the model matrix to construct predictions:

predict_subset <- function(object, newdata, size) {
  beta <- coef(object, id = size)
  Xnew <- model.matrix(y1 ~ ., data = newdata)
  as.vector(Xnew[, names(beta), drop = FALSE] %*% beta)
}

cv_mse <- matrix(NA_real_, nrow = k, ncol = 20)

for (fold in seq_len(k)) {
  fold_train <- development[fold_id != fold, ]
  fold_valid <- development[fold_id == fold, ]

  fold_fit <- regsubsets(
    y1 ~ ., data = fold_train,
    nvmax = 20, method = "exhaustive"
  )

  for (size in seq_len(20)) {
    pred <- predict_subset(fold_fit, fold_valid, size)
    cv_mse[fold, size] <- mean((fold_valid$y1 - pred)^2)
  }
}

# Weight fold MSEs by their validation sample sizes.
fold_sizes <- tabulate(fold_id, nbins = k)
mean_cv_mse <- colSums(cv_mse * fold_sizes) / sum(fold_sizes)
best_size_cv <- which.min(mean_cv_mse)

plot(
  seq_len(20), sqrt(mean_cv_mse), type = "b",
  xlab = "Number of predictors",
  ylab = "Cross-validated RMSE"
)
best_size_cv

# Refit selection using the full development sample.
final_subset <- regsubsets(
  y1 ~ ., data = development,
  nvmax = 20, method = "exhaustive"
)
coef(final_subset, id = best_size_cv)

This code repeats an exhaustive search in every fold, so it can be computationally demanding. In this example, each training portion contains enough observations to fit the candidate sizes, assuming the design matrix is full rank. With fewer observations, the maximum size must be reduced.

Missing-data handling also belongs within the modelling procedure. The complete-case approach above is used to keep the example simple and comparisons on the same observations; it can discard information and change the population represented by the sample. If we use imputation or other learned preprocessing, it should be fitted using each fold’s training observations and then applied to the validation observations.

Backward Stepwise Selection

Exhaustive subset selection becomes expensive very quickly. How can we search fewer candidate models?

In backward stepwise selection, we start with the full ordinary least-squares model, containing all \(p\) predictors. We consider removing each predictor in turn and retain the best model of size \(p-1\). We repeat the process, removing one predictor at each step, until we reach the intercept-only model or meet a specified stopping rule.

The models retained along this path are nested: each smaller model is contained within the preceding larger one. We can then compare the retained models using a selected criterion or validation procedure.

The disadvantage is that the starting full model must be estimable. If there are more predictors than the sample can support, or exact linear dependencies among columns of the design matrix, the ordinary full model does not have a unique set of coefficient estimates.

For 20 predictors, evaluating all possible one-variable deletions along the path requires approximately:

\[ 20+19+\cdots+1=\frac{20(21)}{2}=210 \]

candidate evaluations, in addition to the starting model. This is much smaller than the number of subsets considered in an exhaustive search.

backward_fit <- regsubsets(
  y1 ~ ., data = development,
  nvmax = 20, method = "backward"
)
backward_results <- summary(backward_fit)
which.min(backward_results$bic)

Forward Stepwise Selection

In forward stepwise selection, we start with the null model, containing only an intercept. We consider each available predictor and add the one that gives the best improvement. We then consider adding one of the remaining predictors and repeat the process.

The retained models are again nested. Once a predictor has been included in this forward-only procedure, it remains in the model. This makes the search computationally cheaper than exhaustive selection, but also constrains the combinations we can reach.

The best model of a particular size may lie outside this sequence. A predictor that looks best when added to an intercept-only model need not belong to the best two- or three-predictor combination. The algorithm cannot go back and replace it in a forward-only search.

This does not mean that a stepwise model must predict substantially worse than the exhaustive-search model. Its performance needs to be evaluated. It means that the procedure does not guarantee finding the best subset according to the same criterion.

forward_fit <- regsubsets(
  y1 ~ ., data = development,
  nvmax = 20, method = "forward"
)
forward_results <- summary(forward_fit)

data.frame(
  Adj.R2 = which.max(forward_results$adjr2),
  Cp = which.min(forward_results$cp),
  BIC = which.min(forward_results$bic)
)

plot(forward_fit, scale = "bic")
plot(forward_fit, scale = "adjr2")

In the recorded forward-selection example, adjusted \(R^2\) selects 11 predictors and BIC selects six. The subset plots show which predictors are included at each size and how those candidate models rank according to the chosen criterion.

The lecture also compares prediction error across model sizes. The error falls substantially as the first few predictors are added, but there is little apparent improvement beyond roughly five to seven predictors. This helps us ask whether the added complexity is useful. If that error curve is used to choose the model size, the evaluated observations are acting as validation data.

We can perform the same cross-validation exercise for forward selection by changing method = "exhaustive" to method = "forward" in both the fold fits and the final development fit above. The selection procedure must still be repeated inside each fold.

What Is the Problem with Stepwise Regression?

Stepwise regression is familiar and computationally convenient, but its limitations matter (Harrell, 2001; Gelman, 2014, as discussed in the lecture).

At each step, the candidate models use unconstrained ordinary least squares. We then use the observed data repeatedly to choose among them. The final model is therefore the result of a search, rather than a model fixed before we saw the outcomes.

Conventional p-values from the final fitted model ignore that search. They can be too small, and the corresponding confidence intervals can be too narrow. Predictors selected because they happened to look strong in this sample can also have exaggerated coefficient estimates.

Collinearity adds another problem. When several predictors carry similar information, small changes in the data can change which one is selected. We may therefore obtain different models from closely related samples.

The approach is also described as greedy as it tends towards more complex models.

If our aim is prediction, how can we find a reliable and ideally sparse model? This leads us to the regularisation methods.

L5-2 Multiple Linear Regression: Ridge Regression

On the Issue of Collinearity

Multicollinearity is prominent in many real-world datasets. Opportunistically collected healthcare data and ’omics data often contain variables that describe related aspects of the same process.

In earlier parts, we discussed correlation matrices and variance inflation factors as ways of investigating this problem:

library(car)

full_model <- lm(y1 ~ ., data = development)
vif(full_model)

A variance inflation factor (VIF) describes how much the variance of an estimated coefficient is increased by linear relationships between that predictor and the other predictors. For a single numeric predictor:

\[ \mathrm{VIF}_j=\frac{1}{1-R_j^2}, \]

where \(R_j^2\) is obtained by regressing predictor \(j\) on the remaining predictors.

As a rule of thumb, a VIF above 10 is a warning prompting closer inspection.

NoteDo Predictors Have to Be Independent?

Strong collinearity can make separate coefficient estimates imprecise and unstable (independence of the residual errors is a separate consideration).

Stepwise selection can be particularly unstable when predictors are correlated. One variable may be selected instead of another even though they contain similar information. Ridge regression provides an alternative that retains the predictors while constraining their coefficient estimates.

Shrinkage Methods: Ridge Regression

In ordinary least squares, we minimise the residual sum of squares:

\[ \mathrm{RSS}(\boldsymbol{\beta}) =\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_i-\beta_0-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\beta_jx_{ij}\right)^2. \]

Ridge regression adds a penalty based on the squared coefficients:

\[ \widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{ridge}} =\underset{\beta_0,\boldsymbol{\beta}}{\operatorname{argmin}} \left\{ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_i-\beta_0-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\beta_jx_{ij}\right)^2 +\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{p}\beta_j^2 \right\}. \]

The tuning parameter \(\lambda\) controls the strength of regularisation:

  • At \(\lambda=0\), the objective reduces to ordinary least squares, where its solution is identifiable.
  • As \(\lambda\) increases, the model places a greater penalty on large coefficients.
  • With strong regularisation, the penalised coefficients are drawn towards zero and the model approaches an intercept-only prediction.

The lecture illustrates the penalty using a coefficient of five. Its squared-coefficient contribution is \(5^2=25\), giving a penalty of \(25\lambda\):

beta <- 5
lambda <- c(0, 0.1, 1, 10)

data.frame(
  lambda = lambda,
  ridge_penalty = lambda * beta^2
)
  lambda ridge_penalty
1    0.0           0.0
2    0.1           2.5
3    1.0          25.0
4   10.0         250.0

This calculates the penalty contribution while holding the coefficient fixed. In the fitted model, the coefficient itself changes as the optimisation balances fit against the penalty.

Why Does Ridge Help with Collinearity?

With highly correlated predictors, different combinations of large positive and negative coefficients can produce similar fitted values. Ordinary least squares may therefore estimate coefficients that change substantially when the data change slightly.

The Ridge penalty discourages large coefficients and stabilises these poorly determined directions. Positively correlated predictors can share predictive information rather than one receiving a very large coefficient and another an opposing coefficient.

Centring and Standardising Data

Because the penalty depends on coefficient size, predictor scaling matters. A coefficient expressed per gram can be numerically very different from the same relationship expressed per milligram. Without suitable scaling, the penalty would treat these differently even though the underlying relationship is the same.

For the penalised models in R, we use glmnet’s built-in predictor standardisation. The returned coefficients are on the original predictor scale.

Ridge Regression in R

In the lecture we use the glmnet package. It expects a numeric predictor matrix and a separate outcome vector. The argument alpha = 0 specifies ridge regression, and standardize = TRUE requests predictor standardisation, which is also the default.

library(glmnet)

x <- as.matrix(development[, protein_names])
y <- development$y1

ridge_fit <- glmnet(
  x, y, family = "gaussian",
  alpha = 0, standardize = TRUE
)

ridge_cv <- cv.glmnet(
  x, y, family = "gaussian",
  alpha = 0, standardize = TRUE,
  foldid = fold_id, type.measure = "mse"
)

plot(ridge_fit, xvar = "lambda", label = TRUE)
plot(ridge_cv)

The coefficient-path plot shows how the fitted coefficients change along the regularisation path. Moving towards larger \(\lambda\) means stronger regularisation. In the recorded example, x1 remains prominent while other coefficients become small.

The cross-validation plot shows estimated validation error at different values of \(\lambda\), together with uncertainty represented by standard-error bars. The error may improve initially as regularisation reduces variance, but sufficiently strong shrinkage removes useful predictive information and increases error.

Choosing Lambda and the One-Standard-Error Rule

cv.glmnet() provides two common choices:

Value Meaning
lambda.min The value of \(\lambda\) with the smallest mean cross-validated error.
lambda.1se The largest value of \(\lambda\) whose mean cross-validated error is within one standard error of the minimum.

The one-standard-error rule favours stronger regularisation when the estimated performance is sufficiently close to the minimum. It is a model-selection convention.

ridge_cv$lambda.min
ridge_cv$lambda.1se

coef(ridge_cv, s = "lambda.min")
coef(ridge_cv, s = "lambda.1se")

For Ridge regression, stronger regularisation generally means smaller coefficients rather than fewer included variables. Ridge coefficients are usually nonzero at finite penalties; the squared penalty does not perform variable selection in the way that lasso does. They may be positive or negative. A small coefficient is not evidence that its corresponding protein has no biological role.

So we can stabilise the coefficients, but many remain close to zero. Do we need all of them in a predictive model? This is the question addressed by lasso.

L5-3 Multiple Linear Regression: Lasso Regression

From Small Coefficients to a Sparse Model

In the previous section, Ridge retained all the predictors while shrinking their coefficients. If we want a smaller set of predictors, it would be useful to set some coefficients exactly to zero.

The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator, or Lasso, does this by changing the penalty (Tibshirani, 1996; 1997). The objective in the same teaching convention is:

\[ \widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{lasso}} =\underset{\beta_0,\boldsymbol{\beta}}{\operatorname{argmin}} \left\{ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_i-\beta_0-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\beta_jx_{ij}\right)^2 +\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{p}|\beta_j| \right\}. \]

Ridge penalises the sum of squared coefficients, while lasso penalises the sum of their absolute values. This seemingly small modification allows the optimisation to produce exact zeros.

A coefficient of zero means that the predictor makes no contribution to the prediction equation. Lasso therefore performs both shrinkage and variable selection, creating a sparse model: one in which only some of the candidate predictors have nonzero coefficients.

This connects again to the bias–variance trade-off. A model containing fewer predictors can be easier to use and may have lower prediction variance, at the cost of omitting some relationships. Whether that trade-off improves performance depends on the data and should be evaluated through validation.

Why Can Lasso Produce Exact Zeros?

We can understand the difference by considering how the penalties change as a coefficient approaches zero.

For Ridge, the derivative of the squared-coefficient penalty is:

\[ \frac{d}{d\beta_j}\left(\lambda\beta_j^2\right)=2\lambda\beta_j. \]

Its magnitude becomes smaller as \(\beta_j\) approaches zero. The penalty is smooth at zero and generally shrinks coefficients without setting them exactly to zero.

For Lasso, away from zero:

\[ \frac{d}{d\beta_j}\left(\lambda|\beta_j|\right) =\lambda\operatorname{sign}(\beta_j). \]

The magnitude does not gradually disappear as the coefficient approaches zero. There is a corner in the absolute-value penalty at zero, where the ordinary derivative is not defined. Using the appropriate optimisation condition at that corner, zero can be the optimum when the improvement in fit is too small to overcome the penalty.

This is why we should not describe the Lasso penalty as having an ordinary derivative at zero. The behaviour at that corner is precisely what permits exact zero coefficients.

We use the same package and data structure as for ridge, but set alpha = 1. The same folds allow a more direct comparison of the two methods.

The coefficient-path plot now shows predictors dropping out as their coefficients become zero. With sufficiently strong regularisation, all penalised coefficients can be zero, leaving the intercept-only model.

The cross-validation plot is interpreted in the same way as for ridge. We find the minimum estimated prediction error and can use the one-standard-error rule to choose a more strongly regularised model.

In the lecture example, the one-standard-error choice yields a Lasso model with nine nonzero protein coefficients. By comparison, the ridge output retains coefficients for all 20 proteins. In the printed Lasso output, a dot represents a zero entry in the sparse coefficient matrix.

Lasso and Collinearity

Lasso creates sparse models, but it does not have Ridge’s same stabilising behaviour for groups of highly correlated predictors. It may retain one protein and exclude another that carries very similar information. Small changes in the sample can change which protein is retained.

This matters if we interpret the selected proteins as uniquely important biomarkers. A protein with a zero coefficient may simply have information that another selected protein already captures. Conversely, a nonzero coefficient does not establish a causal mechanism.

Could we combine the ability to stabilise correlated predictors with the ability to create a sparse model? That leads us to elastic net.

L5-4 Multiple Linear Regression: Elastic Net

A Mix of Ridge and Lasso

Elastic net combines the squared-coefficient penalty of ridge with the absolute-value penalty of lasso (Zou and Hastie, 2005).

Using the Gaussian objective convention of glmnet, we can write:

\[ \underset{\beta_0,\boldsymbol{\beta}}{\operatorname{minimise}} \left\{ \frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(y_i-\beta_0-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\beta_jx_{ij}\right)^2 +\lambda\left[ \frac{1-\alpha}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\beta_j^2 +\alpha\sum_{j=1}^{p}|\beta_j| \right] \right\}. \]

There are now two tuning parameters:

  • \(\lambda\) controls the overall strength of regularisation.
  • \(\alpha\) controls the balance between the ridge and lasso components.

The limiting cases are:

\[ \alpha=0:\ \text{ridge}, \qquad \alpha=1:\ \text{lasso}. \]

Values between zero and one combine the two penalties. Elastic net can therefore produce zero coefficients while also providing ridge-like stabilisation for correlated predictors. It is not guaranteed to outperform either endpoint in every dataset. Its value is that we can evaluate the balance appropriate for the prediction task.

Method Penalty component Variable selection Behaviour with correlated predictors
Ridge Squared coefficients Generally retains all predictors Stabilises coefficient estimation and can share information across predictors
Lasso Absolute coefficients Can set coefficients to zero Can select one of several similar predictors; selection may be unstable
Elastic net Combination of both Can set coefficients to zero Combines sparsity with stabilisation of correlated groups

Choosing Alpha and Lambda Through Cross-Validation

We can compare a sequence of \(\alpha\) values, using cross-validation to choose \(\lambda\) for each. We should keep the folds the same across the comparisons so that differences are not simply caused by different random partitions.

The one-standard-error rule here is applied within the selected \(\alpha\) fit. It is not a separate rule for choosing across all \(\alpha\) values. The tuning choices and the candidate grid should be reported clearly.

Interpretability Versus Flexibility

These regularised regression methods sit close to the boundary between familiar statistical regression and machine learning. They remain relatively interpretable because the prediction is an intercept plus a weighted sum of the predictors. More flexible methods can represent more complicated relationships, but their workings may be harder to inspect.

Do we always want the most flexible method? Many times, no. In medicine, we need to verify that the relationships a model has learned are meaningful for the proposed use. We should ask whether it captures relevant information or instead follows spurious associations and confounding in the dataset.

The coefficient of a regularised model remains a weight in its prediction equation. Its magnitude depends on the other predictors, their scaling and the penalty. It should not automatically be interpreted as an unbiased causal effect or as a definitive ranking of biological importance.

Interpretability can help us inspect the model. It does not itself prove that the model is valid, fair or clinically useful. A simple model can still learn a misleading relationship.

A Warning About Bias in Real-World Data

Automated modelling can carry forward biases in the data. Opportunistically collected data reflect the processes through which care, measurement and documentation occur. They do not necessarily represent a controlled experiment or a representative sample of everyone who needs care.

Working with Domain Experts

For clinical data, this means involving clinicians in checking what the variables represent, how the observations were generated and whether the model outputs are plausible.

Experts can help us examine the path from the underlying clinical process to the recorded data and then to the prediction. They may recognise that a variable reflects how often a patient was investigated, how a service was organised, or which patients had access to care, rather than the biological phenomenon we intended to measure.

This involvement goes some way towards identifying problems that a purely statistical assessment may miss. It complements interpretable methods, appropriate validation and continued examination of performance in the intended population.
