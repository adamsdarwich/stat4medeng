NoteKey Takeaways

  • A confusion matrix compares test results with the actual condition, showing true positives, false positives, true negatives and false negatives.

  • Sensitivity describes how well a test detects people with disease. Specificity describes how well the test identifies people without disease.

  • Positive predictive value describes the probability of disease given a positive test. Negative predictive value describes the probability of being disease-free given a negative test. These values depend on disease prevalence.

  • Clinical decisions depend on the benefits and harms of acting on a result. ROC curves help us assess how well a test or classifier distinguishes between the two groups.

L5B Diagnostic Tests

Diagnostic testing is a major part of healthcare, and much of its development comes from engineering and technology research. Diagnostic tools include imaging methods such as X-rays, computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging, as well as biochemical, protein and genetic analyses. We often use these tools to investigate the presence or absence of disease.

A diagnostic tool provides information at an important decision point. A clinician orders an investigation, interprets the result and uses it to inform a diagnosis or treatment decision. Errors can arise throughout this process, so we need to understand the variability and performance of the test. The same applies to classification models, including models developed using machine learning.

Prevalence and Incidence

Prevalence describes the proportion of a population that has a disease at a particular point in time. If we observe ten people and four have influenza, the prevalence is \(4/10=40\%\).

Incidence describes new cases arising during a specified time period. For example, if ten people are initially flu-free and two develop influenza during follow-up, 20% develop the disease over that period (e.g., cases per 10 people per week).

For the diagnostic calculations below, prevalence is particularly relevant. We will see that how common a disease is affects how we interpret a test result.

The Confusion Matrix

A contingency table summarises the frequencies for combinations of categorical variables. A confusion matrix compares predicted classes with the actual or reference classes. For a binary diagnostic test, we compare positive and negative test results with disease present and disease absent.

Actual condition Positive test Negative test
Disease present True positive (TP) False negative (FN)
Disease absent False positive (FP) True negative (TN)

A true positive is a positive result in someone who has the disease. A false positive is a positive result in someone who does not. A false negative is a negative result in someone who has the disease, while a true negative is a negative result in someone who does not.

The positions of the cells can differ between software packages. Always read the row and column labels and check which class is considered positive.

A Diagnostic Test Example

Suppose we test 150 people. Thirty have the disease and 120 do not:

Actual condition Positive test Negative test Total
Disease present 25 5 30
Disease absent 15 105 120
Total 40 110 150

We can use these four cells to calculate several measures of performance. Each measure answers a different question.

Accuracy

How often is the test correct? Accuracy is the proportion of all results that are true positives or true negatives:

\[ \text{Accuracy}=\frac{TP+TN}{TP+FN+FP+TN} =\frac{25+105}{150}\approx87\%. \]

Accuracy can be misleading when the classes are unbalanced. If most people do not have the disease, a classifier can achieve high accuracy simply by classifying everyone as negative. We therefore need to examine how the test performs within each group.

Sensitivity and Specificity

Sensitivity

How likely is a person with disease to be correctly classified? Sensitivity is the probability of a positive test given that the person has the disease:

\[ \text{Sensitivity}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN} =\frac{25}{30}\approx83\%. \]

The test identifies 25 of the 30 people with disease and misses five.

Specificity

How likely is a person without disease to be correctly classified? Specificity is the probability of a negative test given that the person does not have the disease:

\[ \text{Specificity}=\frac{TN}{TN+FP} =\frac{105}{120}\approx88\%. \]

The test correctly identifies 105 of the 120 people without disease. The remaining 15 receive false positive results.

Sensitivity and specificity start with the person’s actual condition and describe the likely test result. We can also ask the question in the other direction: given a test result, how likely is the person to have the disease?

Positive and Negative Predictive Values

Positive Predictive Value

How likely is someone with a positive test to actually have the disease? This is the positive predictive value, or PPV:

\[ \text{PPV}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP} =\frac{25}{40}\approx63\%. \]

Of the 40 people with positive results, 25 have the disease. So, approximately 63% of positive results represent disease, even though sensitivity is approximately 83%.

We can also calculate PPV from sensitivity, specificity and prevalence (based on Bayes’ theorem):

\[ \text{PPV}= \frac{\text{Sensitivity}\times\text{Prevalence}} {\text{Sensitivity}\times\text{Prevalence} +(1-\text{Specificity})\times(1-\text{Prevalence})}. \]

The numerator represents true positives as a proportion of the population. The denominator includes both true positives and false positives. This is the same calculation as dividing the number of true positives by all positive results.

Negative Predictive Value

How likely is someone with a negative test to actually be disease-free? This is the negative predictive value, or NPV:

\[ \text{NPV}=\frac{TN}{TN+FN} =\frac{105}{110}\approx95\%. \]

Of the 110 people with negative results, 105 do not have the disease.

NoteKeeping the Questions Separate
  • Sensitivity: among people with disease, how many test positive?
  • Specificity: among people without disease, how many test negative?
  • PPV: among people with a positive test, how many have disease?
  • NPV: among people with a negative test, how many are disease-free?

Generating a Confusion Matrix in R

We can construct a hypothetical dataset using sensitivity, specificity and the numbers of people with and without disease. In this example, sensitivity is 0.98 and specificity is 0.95, with 1,000 people in each group:

sens <- 0.98
spec <- 0.95
n_diseased <- 1000
n_healthy <- 1000

TP <- round(sens * n_diseased)
FN <- n_diseased - TP
TN <- round(spec * n_healthy)
FP <- n_healthy - TN

actual <- factor(c(rep(1, TP), rep(1, FN), rep(0, TN), rep(0, FP)))
predicted <- factor(c(rep(1, TP), rep(0, FN), rep(0, TN), rep(1, FP)))

caret::confusionMatrix(
  data = predicted,
  reference = actual,
  positive = "1"
)

What Happens When Disease Is Less Common?

Return to our original example. Suppose we keep the number of people with disease unchanged but multiply the number without disease by ten:

Actual condition Positive test Negative test Total
Disease present 25 5 30
Disease absent 150 1,050 1,200
Total 175 1,055 1,230

Sensitivity remains \(25/30\approx83\%\), and specificity remains \(1050/1200\approx88\%\). However, prevalence has fallen from 20% to approximately 2.4%.

The positive predictive value is now:

\[ \text{PPV}=\frac{25}{175}\approx14\%. \]

The test still detects the same proportion of disease cases. However, the much larger group without disease produces many more false positives. Most positive results now come from people without disease.

The negative predictive value increases:

\[ \text{NPV}=\frac{1050}{1055}\approx99.5\%. \]

Predictive values depend on disease prevalence. In this example, sensitivity and specificity remain unchanged because we have kept the same proportions within the disease and disease-free groups.

Likelihood Ratios, Odds and Log Odds

A positive likelihood ratio describes how much more likely a positive test is in someone with disease than in someone without disease:

\[ LR+=\frac{\text{Sensitivity}}{1-\text{Specificity}}. \]

A negative likelihood ratio compares the probability of a negative test in someone with disease with that in someone without disease:

\[ LR-=\frac{1-\text{Sensitivity}}{\text{Specificity}}. \]

For a useful test, a positive result increases our assessment of the probability of disease, while a negative result decreases it.

In the lecture I also describe this using odds. A probability \(p\) corresponds to odds of \(p/(1-p)\). On the odds scale, the test result multiplies the pre-test odds by the relevant likelihood ratio. For a useful test, this multiplier is above one for a positive result and below one for a negative result.

On the log-odds scale, these changes become additive. A positive result moves the log odds upwards and a negative result moves them downwards. This resembles logistic regression, where each term contributes a change on the log-odds scale.

Clinical Thresholds

A test result informs a decision, but the decision also depends on its consequences. Treatment may benefit someone with disease, while also causing adverse effects. We therefore need to weigh the expected benefits against the harms.

If the harms of treatment become more severe, we would generally require a higher probability of disease before treating. If the treatment benefit becomes greater, treatment may be justified at a lower probability. The treatment threshold therefore depends on the clinical situation.

ROC Curves

Diagnostic tests and classification models may produce a continuous measurement or score. We choose a cutoff to classify the result as positive or negative. Changing this cutoff changes sensitivity and specificity.

A receiver operating characteristic, or ROC, curve plots sensitivity against \(1-\text{specificity}\) across different cutoffs. The horizontal axis therefore shows the proportion of people without disease who receive a positive result.

A curve closer to the top-left corner indicates better performance: high sensitivity with few false positives. The diagonal represents performance no better than chance.

The area under the ROC curve, or AUROC, summarises how well the test distinguishes between people with and without disease. An area of 1 indicates perfect separation, while 0.5 indicates performance no better than chance. It does not tell us the probability of disease after a positive result; that is the PPV.

We will examine ROC curves in more detail later in the course.

Other Terms

In machine learning, sensitivity is often called recall, and positive predictive value is called precision. They are the same measures under different names.

In the video I also introduce Youden’s index. This can help select a cut-off by maximising sensitivity and specificity.
